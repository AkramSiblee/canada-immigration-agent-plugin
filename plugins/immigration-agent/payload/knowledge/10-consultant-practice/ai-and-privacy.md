# AI tools and privacy in RCIC practice

## Must cover
- CICC position, confidentiality, client consent, supervision, privacy law, cross-border storage, court and tribunal AI rules

## Current rules
### CICC position (M)
- **No AI-specific CICC rule or guidance found** as of 2026-11-09. Absence is not proof; confirm with the College.
- The College told New Canadian Media (reported 2026) that licensees must meet their duties "competently and diligently, in line with the Code of Professional Conduct, regardless of the tools they may use".
- Board package, September 2025: a scoping review of how regulators approach AI is complete, so the College can offer guidance "should AI become part of immigration practice". Guidance may follow.

### Code duties that apply to AI use (SOR/2022-128) (H for text; M for application to AI)
| Section | Duty | Effect on AI use |
|---|---|---|
| s.19(1), (2)(e) | Competence; deliver services using appropriate and effective technology | Understand the tool and its limits. Verify output |
| s.24(q) | Service agreement states the confidentiality duty and **how** confidentiality will be maintained | Describe AI and cloud handling in the agreement |
| s.28(1) | Keep client information confidential indefinitely, with necessary measures | Applies to prompts, uploads, logs and cloud sync |
| s.28(2)(a), (e) | Disclosure only if authorized by the client, or to a person assisting the licensee, among other grounds | Whether an AI vendor is a "person assisting" is **untested**. Get client authorization |
| s.38(1)-(2) | Supervise and take professional responsibility for those assisting | Applied by analogy: review all AI output |
| s.38(3) | Non-licensees can't give advice contrary to IRPA s.91 | Public chatbots must not give individualized immigration advice |

### SOR/2026-68: correction
- ss.41 and 46-50 govern what the **College, Registrar and committees** may obtain or disclose. s.50 (foreign entities) binds the College.
- They do **not** set a rule for a consultant's cloud or AI vendors. Earlier notes in this knowledge base implied otherwise.

### Immigration and Refugee Board: Practice Notice on AI (in force 2026-09-07; page 2026-09-08) (H)
Applies to all four divisions and all parties and counsel.
- **Banned:** using AI to generate or materially change a person's evidence: basis of claim narratives, affidavits, witness statements, rewriting a person's story, or creating or altering photos, videos, screenshots or other corroborating evidence. Spellcheck, grammar and formatting are allowed if they don't make material changes.
- **Required declaration** in any document where AI generated or materially changed content: "Artificial intelligence (AI) was used to create or materially change text in this document. All content generated by AI, and the authenticity of any cited case law or other legal authorities, has been reviewed and verified by [name]."
- Verify law and citations against reliable sources such as CanLII. AI summaries inside those sources don't count.
- AI use for transcription or translation must always be disclosed; a human translator's declaration is still required and must state any AI assistance.
- Be ready to answer questions about AI use. Don't pad submissions. Take care with sensitive information.
- **Consequences:** document refused or not relied on, negative credibility inferences, report to the regulator, restricting or prohibiting counsel.

### Federal Court: Notice of 2024-05-07 (H)
- A declaration in the first paragraph is required when material prepared for litigation contains AI-generated content. Lawyer-only forum for RCICs; flag for referral.

### IRCC applications
- No IRCC rule requiring disclosure of AI use by applicants or representatives was found (L). Accuracy duties and IRPA s.40 still apply. Don't assume the IRB ban extends to IRCC, but treat a client's own statements as the client's words.

### Privacy law (H)
- **PIPEDA** covers private-sector commercial activity, and information crossing provincial or national borders. Organizations operating entirely within **Alberta, BC or Quebec** fall under those provinces' general private-sector laws for activity there. (OPC page 2018-01-31.)
- **Accountability (Sch.1, 4.1.3):** the organization stays responsible for information sent to a third party for processing and must use contractual or other means to provide a comparable level of protection.
- **Consent (4.3.4):** the form depends on sensitivity. Medical and income records are almost always sensitive. Immigration files also hold identity and family data.
- **Cross-border (OPC 2009):** transfer abroad isn't prohibited, but the organization remains accountable. Tell clients their data may be processed abroad and may be accessible under foreign law.
- **Breaches (s.10.1):** report to the Commissioner and notify individuals where there is a real risk of significant harm.
- **OPC generative AI principles (page 2025-05-06):** valid and meaningful consent, purpose limits, safeguards, accountability, and extra care with sensitive information. A 2026 joint OpenAI finding held that express consent was needed where information was sensitive or outside reasonable expectations.
- **Quebec (P-39.1 s.17):** a privacy impact assessment is required **before** sending personal information outside Quebec or entrusting it to someone outside Quebec, plus a written agreement. Quebec consultants have a harder rule.

## Consultant pitfalls
- Never have AI draft or rewrite a BOC narrative, affidavit or witness statement for the IRB.
- Include the IRB declaration on any IRB document AI drafted or materially changed, naming the person who verified it.
- Verify every case citation at source.
- Get written client authorization before putting identifiable information into an AI tool or cloud service.
- A consumer AI plan with training on is not suitable for client files.
- Use a business storage and AI plan with a written data agreement for real client data.
